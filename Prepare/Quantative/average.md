# 1. Introduction

Average is one of the simplest yet most frequently tested topics in Quantitative Aptitude. It represents a single value that best describes a group of numbers.

In everyday language, average means "the typical value" of a set of numbers.

Mathematically:

Average = (Sum of Observations) / (Number of Observations)

For example, if three friends scored 70, 80, and 90 marks, their average score is:

(70+80+90)/3 = 240/3 = 80

So 80 represents a single number that fairly summarizes the group's performance.

Average is used everywhere — average marks, average speed, average income, average rainfall, average temperature — making it a highly practical and frequently examined topic.

# 3. Key Concepts

## 3.1 Basic Average

Average = Sum of all observations / Number of observations

## 3.2 Average of Natural Numbers

Average of first n natural numbers = (n+1)/2

## 3.3 Average of Squares of first n Natural Numbers

= (n+1)(2n+1)/6

## 3.4 Average of Consecutive Numbers

If numbers are consecutive (like 5,6,7,8), the average is simply the middle value (or the average of the two middle values if the count is even).

## 3.5 Effect of Including/Excluding a Value

- When a new value is added to a group, if it is greater than the current average, the new average increases; if smaller, it decreases.
- When a value is removed, the opposite logic applies.

## 3.6 Effect of Replacement

When one observation is replaced by another, the change in average depends only on the difference between the new and old value, divided by the number of observations.

## 3.7 Weighted Average

Used when different groups have different sizes.

Weighted Average = (n1×a1 + n2×a2) / (n1+n2)

Where n1, n2 are the number of items in each group and a1, a2 are their respective averages.

## 3.8 Average Speed

When distance is constant but speed varies for two parts of a journey:

Average Speed = 2×S1×S2 / (S1+S2)

This is the harmonic mean of the two speeds, NOT the simple average.

# 4. Formula Sheet

| Concept | Formula |
|---------|---------|
| Basic Average | Sum / Count |
| Sum from Average | Average × Count |
| Average of first n natural numbers | (n+1)/2 |
| Average of first n even numbers | (n+1) |
| Average of first n odd numbers | n |
| Average of squares of first n natural numbers | (n+1)(2n+1)/6 |
| New Average after inclusion | (Old Sum + New Value)/(Old Count+1) |
| New Average after exclusion | (Old Sum − Removed Value)/(Old Count−1) |
| Change in Average due to Replacement | (New Value − Old Value)/Number of Observations |
| Weighted Average (2 groups) | (n1a1+n2a2)/(n1+n2) |
| Average Speed (equal distance, 2 speeds) | 2S1S2/(S1+S2) |
| Average Speed (equal time, 2 speeds) | (S1+S2)/2 |

# 5. Memory Tricks

**Trick 1 — Deviation Method**

Instead of adding all numbers directly, assume a base value close to the numbers, find deviations from that base, average the deviations, then add back to the base. This is much faster for large or close numbers.

**Trick 2 — Average of Consecutive Numbers**

For consecutive numbers, the average is always the middle number (odd count) or the average of the two middle numbers (even count) — no need to add everything.

**Trick 3 — Replacement Shortcut**

When one value replaces another, the total change in average = (difference in values) ÷ (number of people). You never need to recompute the entire sum.

**Trick 4 — Average Speed Trap**

Whenever a question says "equal distance covered at two speeds," always use the harmonic mean formula 2S1S2/(S1+S2). Never average the two speeds directly — this is one of the most common mistakes.

**Trick 5 — New Person Joining/Leaving**

If a new person's value is above the group average, the average rises; if below, it falls. This logical check helps verify your final answer.

# 6. Solved Examples

## Example 1

Find the average of 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36.

**Solution**

Sum = 12+18+24+30+36 = 120

Average = 120/5 = 24

**Answer:** 24

---

## Example 2

The average age of 30 students in a class is 12 years. If the teacher's age is included, the average becomes 13 years. Find the teacher's age.

**Solution**

Sum of students' ages = 30×12 = 360

Sum including teacher = 31×13 = 403

Teacher's age = 403−360 = 43

**Answer:** 43 years

---

## Example 3

The average of 5 numbers is 20. If one number is replaced by 35, the new average becomes 23. Find the number that was replaced.

**Solution**

Increase in total = (23−20) × 5 = 15

Increase in value = 35 − Old Number = 15

Old Number = 35−15 = 20

**Answer:** 20

---

## Example 4

A car covers half the distance at 40 km/h and the other half at 60 km/h. Find the average speed for the entire journey.

**Solution**

Average Speed = 2×40×60/(40+60) = 4800/100 = 48 km/h

**Answer:** 48 km/h

---

## Example 5

The average marks of 40 students in a class is 60. If the average of 25 of them is 70, find the average of the remaining 15 students.

**Solution**

Total marks of 40 students = 40×60 = 2400

Total marks of 25 students = 25×70 = 1750

Total marks of remaining 15 = 2400−1750 = 650

Average of remaining 15 = 650/15 = 43.33

**Answer:** 43.33 (approximately)

# 7. Advanced Questions

## Advanced Question 1

The average weight of 10 boys is increased by 2 kg when one boy weighing 45 kg is replaced by a new boy. Find the weight of the new boy.

**Solution**

Increase in total weight = 2×10 = 20 kg

New boy's weight = 45+20 = 65 kg

**Answer:** 65 kg

---

## Advanced Question 2

The average of 11 results is 50. If the average of the first 6 results is 49 and that of the last 6 results is 52, find the 6th result.

**Solution**

Sum of 11 results = 11×50 = 550

Sum of first 6 = 6×49 = 294

Sum of last 6 = 6×52 = 312

Sum of first 6 + last 6 = 294+312 = 606

Since the 6th result is counted twice (once in each group):

6th result = 606 − 550 = 56

**Answer:** 56

---

## Advanced Question 3

A student's average marks in 5 subjects is 84. After re-evaluation of one subject, the average becomes 86. By how much did the marks in that subject increase?

**Solution**

Increase in total = (86−84)×5 = 10

**Answer:** Marks increased by 10

---

## Advanced Question 4

The average age of a family of 6 members is 22 years. If the age of the youngest member is 7 years, find the average age of the family at the time of the birth of the youngest member (assume no one else was born or left during this time).

**Solution**

Present total age of all 6 members = 6×22 = 132

Present total age of the other 5 members (excluding the youngest) = 132−7 = 125

7 years ago, each of these 5 members was 7 years younger, so their total age then was:

125 − (5×7) = 125−35 = 90

At that time, the youngest member was not yet born, so the family had only these 5 members.

Average age of the family then = 90/5 = 18 years

**Answer:** 18 years

# 8. Common Mistakes

❌ Averaging two speeds directly instead of using the harmonic mean formula for equal-distance journeys.

❌ Forgetting to adjust the count of observations when a value is added or removed.

❌ Confusing "average increases" with "average of the new value" — the increase applies to the whole group, not just the new entrant.

❌ Not accounting for double-counted terms in overlapping average problems (as seen in Advanced Question 2).

❌ Ignoring the sign of change (increase vs decrease) when calculating replacement problems.

❌ Assuming average always equals the middle value — this is only true for evenly spaced (consecutive/arithmetic) data.

# 9. Interview Questions

### Q1. What is the difference between Average and Weighted Average?

A simple average treats all values equally, while a weighted average accounts for the relative size or importance (weight) of each group before combining them.

### Q2. Why can't we simply average two speeds to find average speed?

Because speed is a rate (distance/time), and averaging rates directly is only valid when time is constant, not distance. For equal distances, the harmonic mean must be used instead.

### Q3. What happens to the average when a value equal to the current average is added?

The average remains unchanged, since the added value does not shift the balance of the group.

### Q4. Can the average of a data set be higher than all its individual values?

No, the average of a data set always lies between its minimum and maximum values.

### Q5. How is average related to statistics as a subject?

Average is the arithmetic mean, one of the central measures of tendency in statistics, alongside median and mode.
