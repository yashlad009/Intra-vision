# 1. Introduction

Time and Work is one of the most frequently tested topics in placement aptitude exams, and it builds directly on the concept of **Ratio and Proportion** and **Percentage**.

The topic deals with a simple real-world situation:

If a person, a machine, or a group of people can complete a certain amount of work in a certain amount of time, how do we calculate:

- How much work is done in a given time?
- How long will it take to finish the work?
- How does the time change when more people join or leave?
- How do we handle situations where people work at different speeds?

At its core, Time and Work is simply an application of the **unitary method** — a concept you have used since school, now applied to more complex, exam-style situations.

Once you understand the basic logic behind this topic, you will realize that almost every question — no matter how complicated it looks — is solved using the same core idea:

> "Work done is directly related to the time spent and the rate at which work is done."

# 3. Key Concepts

## 3.1 The Basic Idea: Work as a Unit

In Time and Work problems, we treat the **total work** as **1 unit** (or sometimes as a convenient number like the LCM of given times, to avoid fractions).

If a person can complete a piece of work in **n days**, then:

Work done by that person in 1 day = 1/n

This fraction 1/n is called the person's **rate of work**, or their **efficiency**.

---

## 3.2 The Golden Relationship

The entire chapter revolves around one central relationship:

Work = Rate × Time

This is exactly analogous to:

Distance = Speed × Time

In fact, many students find it easier to understand Time and Work by mentally replacing:

- "Work" with "Distance"
- "Rate of work" with "Speed"
- "Time taken" with "Time taken"

---

## 3.3 Combined Work (Working Together)

When two or more people work together, their **rates of work simply add up**.

If A can do a work in **a days** and B can do the same work in **b days**, then:

A's one day work = 1/a

B's one day work = 1/b

Combined one day work = 1/a + 1/b

Time taken together = 1 / (1/a + 1/b) = ab / (a + b)

This is one of the most important formulas in the entire chapter. Memorize it well.

---

## 3.4 Efficiency and Time are Inversely Proportional

This is a concept many students overlook, but it is critical.

If Person A is **twice as efficient** as Person B, then A takes **half the time** B takes to complete the same work.

In general:

Efficiency ∝ 1 / Time

So if the efficiency ratio of A and B is given as **a : b**, then the ratio of time taken by A and B is **b : a** (the ratio gets inverted).

---

## 3.5 Negative Work (People Leaving / Undoing Work)

Sometimes a question involves someone undoing the work already done — such as one pipe filling a tank and another pipe emptying it. In such cases, we subtract the rate of the person doing negative work instead of adding it.

Net rate = (Sum of rates of people helping) − (Sum of rates of people opposing)

This concept becomes especially important in the **Pipes and Cisterns** extension of this chapter.

---

## 3.6 Man-Days Concept

This is used when the number of workers changes during the work, or when we compare work done by different numbers of people over different durations.

Total Work = Number of Men × Number of Days (assuming each man works at the same rate, and works the same number of hours per day)

This is often written as:

M₁D₁ = M₂D₂ (for the same amount of work)

If working hours per day are also different, we extend this to:

M₁ D₁ H₁ = M₂ D₂ H₂

Where H represents hours worked per day.

If the work amount is also different (say W₁ and W₂ units of work), the formula becomes:

(M₁ D₁ H₁) / W₁ = (M₂ D₂ H₂) / W₂

This is the most general and powerful formula in the chapter, and a large number of advanced questions are solved using this single equation.

# 5. Memory Tricks

## Trick 1 — LCM Method (Avoiding Fractions Entirely)

Instead of assuming total work = 1, assume total work = **LCM of the given days**.

This converts all fractional rates into whole numbers, which are far easier to calculate mentally.

Example

A can do a work in 10 days, B can do it in 15 days.

LCM of 10 and 15 = 30

Assume Total Work = 30 units

A's efficiency = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 units/day

B's efficiency = 30 ÷ 15 = 2 units/day

Combined efficiency = 3 + 2 = 5 units/day

Time taken together = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 days

This method eliminates fractions completely and is the preferred method used by most fast solvers.

---

## Trick 2 — Efficiency Ratio Shortcut

If A is thrice as efficient as B:

Efficiency ratio A : B = 3 : 1

Time ratio A : B = 1 : 3 (inverted)

If A takes 4 days, B takes 4 × 3 = 12 days.

---

## Trick 3 — Combined Work Percentage Method

Convert each person's one day work into a percentage of total work. This is especially useful for verbal/mental calculation without paper.

Example

A finishes work in 20 days → 5% per day

B finishes work in 25 days → 4% per day

Combined = 9% per day

Time = 100 ÷ 9 ≈ 11.1 days

---

## Trick 4 — Remember the "Reciprocal Relationship"

Whenever "days to finish" is given, immediately think of its reciprocal as "work done per day." This mental switch is the single most useful habit in this chapter.

# 6. Solved Examples

## Example 1 — Basic Time and Work

A can complete a piece of work in 12 days. Find A's one day work.

### Solution

A's one day work = 1/12

### Answer

1/12

---

## Example 2 — Work Done in Given Days

If A can complete a work in 15 days, how much work will A complete in 5 days?

### Solution

A's one day work = 1/15

Work done in 5 days = 5 × (1/15) = 5/15 = 1/3

### Answer

1/3 of the total work

---

## Example 3 — Two People Working Together

A can do a work in 10 days and B can do the same work in 15 days. In how many days will they finish the work together?

### Solution

A's one day work = 1/10

B's one day work = 1/15

Combined one day work = 1/10 + 1/15

LCM of 10 and 15 = 30

= 3/30 + 2/30

= 5/30

= 1/6

Time taken together = 6 days

### Answer

6 days

---

## Example 4 — Three People Working Together

A, B and C can complete a work in 20, 30 and 60 days respectively. Find the time taken if all three work together.

### Solution

Using LCM method:

LCM of 20, 30, 60 = 60

A's efficiency = 60/20 = 3

B's efficiency = 60/30 = 2

C's efficiency = 60/60 = 1

Combined efficiency = 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 units/day

Time taken = 60 ÷ 6 = 10 days

### Answer

10 days

---

## Example 5 — Efficiency Comparison

A is twice as efficient as B. If B can complete a work in 24 days, find the time taken by A.

### Solution

Efficiency ratio A : B = 2 : 1

Time ratio A : B = 1 : 2 (inverted)

Since B takes 24 days,

A takes 24 ÷ 2 = 12 days

### Answer

12 days

---

## Example 6 — Work Left After Some Days

A and B together can complete a work in 12 days. They work together for 4 days, after which A leaves. B alone completes the remaining work in 16 days. Find B's total time to complete the work alone.

### Solution

Combined one day work of A and B = 1/12

Work done in 4 days = 4/12 = 1/3

Remaining work = 1 − 1/3 = 2/3

B completes 2/3 of work in 16 days

B's one day work = (2/3) ÷ 16 = 2/48 = 1/24

So B alone takes 24 days to complete the work.

### Answer

24 days

---

## Example 7 — Man-Days Concept

If 15 men can complete a work in 20 days, how many men are needed to complete the same work in 12 days?

### Solution

Using M₁D₁ = M₂D₂

15 × 20 = M₂ × 12

300 = M₂ × 12

M₂ = 300 ÷ 12 = 25

### Answer

25 men

---

## Example 8 — Man-Days with Hours

12 men working 8 hours a day can complete a work in 10 days. How many days will 10 men working 6 hours a day take to complete the same work?

### Solution

Using M₁D₁H₁ = M₂D₂H₂

12 × 10 × 8 = 10 × D₂ × 6

960 = 60 × D₂

D₂ = 16 days

### Answer

16 days

---

## Example 9 — Wages Distribution

A and B undertake a work for ₹3300. A alone can do the work in 8 days, B alone can do it in 12 days. With the help of C, they complete the work in 4 days. Find C's share.

### Solution

A's one day work = 1/8

B's one day work = 1/12

Combined (A+B) one day work = 1/8 + 1/12 = 3/24 + 2/24 = 5/24

Combined (A+B+C) one day work (since together they finish in 4 days) = 1/4

C's one day work = 1/4 − 5/24 = 6/24 − 5/24 = 1/24

Ratio of work done by A : B : C = 1/8 : 1/12 : 1/24

Multiply by 24 (LCM) → 3 : 2 : 1

Total parts = 6

C's share = (1/6) × 3300 = ₹550

### Answer

₹550

---

## Example 10 — Alternate Days Working

A can complete a work in 10 days and B can complete it in 15 days. They work on alternate days, starting with A. In how many days will the work be completed?

### Solution

A's one day work = 1/10

B's one day work = 1/15

Work done in 2 days (A then B) = 1/10 + 1/15 = 3/30 + 2/30 = 5/30 = 1/6

In 2 days, 1/6 of work is done.

In 12 days (6 such cycles), work done = 6 × 1/6 = 1 (i.e., work is fully done in 12 days)

But we must check exactly, since work may complete partway through a cycle. Let's verify:

After 10 days (5 cycles): work done = 5 × 1/6 = 5/6

Remaining work = 1/6

Day 11 is A's turn → A does 1/10 of work

1/10 compared with 1/6: since 1/10 < 1/6, work is not finished on day 11.

Remaining after day 11 = 1/6 − 1/10 = 5/30 − 3/30 = 2/30 = 1/15

Day 12 is B's turn → B does 1/15 of work per day, which exactly matches the remaining 1/15.

So work finishes exactly on Day 12.

### Answer

12 days

# 7. Advanced Questions

## Advanced Question 1 — Combined Pipes and Work Style Problem

A, B and C can complete a work in 6, 8 and 12 days respectively. All three start together, but A leaves after 2 days and B leaves 3 days before the work is completed. In how many days is the work completed?

### Solution

Let total work = LCM(6,8,12) = 24 units

A's efficiency = 24/6 = 4 units/day

B's efficiency = 24/8 = 3 units/day

C's efficiency = 24/12 = 2 units/day

Let total time = D days.

A works for 2 days → contributes 4 × 2 = 8 units

B works for (D − 3) days → contributes 3 × (D − 3)

C works for D days (works throughout) → contributes 2 × D

Total work = 8 + 3(D − 3) + 2D = 24

8 + 3D − 9 + 2D = 24

5D − 1 = 24

5D = 25

D = 5

### Answer

5 days

---

## Advanced Question 2 — Reverse Efficiency Problem

A can complete a work in 'x' days and B in 'x + 6' days. If together they complete the work in 4 days, find x.

### Solution

1/x + 1/(x+6) = 1/4

Taking LCM:

[(x+6) + x] / [x(x+6)] = 1/4

(2x + 6) / (x² + 6x) = 1/4

Cross-multiplying:

4(2x + 6) = x² + 6x

8x + 24 = x² + 6x

x² − 2x − 24 = 0

(x − 6)(x + 4) = 0

x = 6 (rejecting the negative value)

### Answer

x = 6, so A takes 6 days and B takes 12 days.

---

## Advanced Question 3 — Work and Wages with Unequal Rates

A can do a work in 20 days and B is 25% more efficient than A. If they work together, find the number of days taken and the ratio in which they should share wages of ₹9000.

### Solution

A's efficiency = 1/20

B is 25% more efficient → B's efficiency = 1.25 × (1/20) = 1/16

Combined efficiency = 1/20 + 1/16

LCM of 20 and 16 = 80

= 4/80 + 5/80 = 9/80

Time taken = 80/9 days ≈ 8.89 days

Wage Ratio = Efficiency Ratio = 4 : 5 (from the numerators above)

Total parts = 9

A's share = (4/9) × 9000 = ₹4000

B's share = (5/9) × 9000 = ₹5000

### Answer

Time = 80/9 days; A gets ₹4000, B gets ₹5000

---

## Advanced Question 4 — Variable Workforce (Advanced Man-Days)

A contractor employs 50 workers to complete a project in 30 days. After 10 days, 20 more workers join. In how many total days will the project finish?

### Solution

Total work = 50 × 30 = 1500 man-days

Work done in first 10 days = 50 × 10 = 500 man-days

Remaining work = 1500 − 500 = 1000 man-days

New workforce = 50 + 20 = 70 workers

Days needed for remaining work = 1000 ÷ 70 ≈ 14.29 days

Total time = 10 + 14.29 ≈ 24.29 days

### Answer

Approximately 24.3 days (24 days and a few hours)

# 8. Common Mistakes

❌ Assuming total work = 1 for every question, even when it's easier to use LCM to avoid fractions.

❌ Forgetting that efficiency and time are inversely proportional, not directly proportional.

❌ Adding time taken instead of adding rates when people work together.

Example of wrong thinking: "A takes 10 days, B takes 15 days, so together they take 10+15=25 days" — this is completely incorrect.

❌ Using the man-days formula without accounting for different working hours per day when the question specifies them.

❌ Forgetting to subtract the rate of a person doing negative work (like an outlet pipe or someone undoing the work).

❌ Mixing up work-based wage ratio with time-based wage ratio. Wages are always distributed according to the **amount of work done**, not simply time spent.

❌ Not verifying alternate-day or partial-day problems carefully — the work may finish partway through a cycle, and blindly multiplying cycles gives the wrong answer.

# 9. Interview Questions

### Q1

What is the fundamental relationship in Time and Work problems?

Work = Rate × Time. This is the foundational equation for the entire chapter.

---

### Q2

How are efficiency and time related?

They are inversely proportional. Higher efficiency always means less time required for the same amount of work.

---

### Q3

Why do we use the LCM method in Time and Work?

To avoid dealing with fractions during calculation, making mental math faster and more accurate.

---

### Q4

What happens to the combined rate when a person is undoing work (like an outlet pipe)?

Their rate is subtracted from the total combined rate instead of being added.

---

### Q5

How is wage distribution decided among workers in a joint project?

Wages are distributed in proportion to the actual work contributed by each person, which depends on both their efficiency and the number of days they worked.

# Key Takeaways

• Time and Work is governed by the relationship Work = Rate × Time.

• Efficiency and time taken are always inversely proportional.

• The LCM method is the fastest way to avoid fractional calculations.

• The Man-Days formula extends naturally to include working hours and differing amounts of work.

• Wages should always be distributed based on actual work contributed, not merely time spent.
